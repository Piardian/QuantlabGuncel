# CSM-001 / LR-001 Cross-Sectional Momentum Literature Review

## Purpose

This review synthesizes the scientific literature on Cross-Sectional Momentum as an expected-return mechanism. It does not define the frozen CSM-001 construct, select parameters, choose implementation details, run tests, claim alpha, or recommend trading strategies.

## Primary Research Question

What does the current scientific literature collectively conclude about Cross-Sectional Momentum as an independent expected-return mechanism?

## High-Level Conclusion

Cross-Sectional Momentum is one of the most mature and widely studied empirical regularities in asset pricing. The literature supports it as a serious independent edge-mechanism candidate, especially in U.S. equities and broader global/cross-asset evidence. However, the literature also documents important qualifications: definition sensitivity, transaction costs, crashes, post-publication decay, multiple-testing concerns, and incomplete agreement on mechanism.

## Established Scientific Consensus

- Momentum is typically studied by ranking assets cross-sectionally by medium-horizon prior returns and comparing winners with losers.
- The foundational U.S. equity evidence begins with Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), who documented continuation among past winners versus losers over intermediate horizons.
- Carhart (1997) made momentum a standard factor in mutual fund performance evaluation by adding a momentum factor to the Fama-French framework.
- Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013) show that value and momentum effects appear across multiple markets and asset classes, though this broader evidence is not identical to U.S. equity CSM.
- The literature does not identify a single universally accepted explanation; behavioral, risk-based, liquidity and institutional explanations remain active.

## Competing Hypotheses

- Behavioral underreaction: prices adjust slowly to information, causing past winners to keep winning over intermediate horizons.
- Delayed overreaction: continuation can eventually reverse, producing long-run reversal or crash-prone behavior.
- Risk compensation: momentum profits may reflect exposure to systematic risks, funding liquidity, crash risk or other state variables.
- Institutional frictions: slow capital movement, delegated management, benchmark constraints and limits to arbitrage may sustain continuation.

## Unresolved Questions

- Which operational definition best captures the economically relevant momentum construct?
- How much of the historical premium survives realistic costs and capacity?
- Are post-publication returns structurally lower?
- How should crash risk be modeled and managed?
- How much evidence is robust after multiple-testing corrections and stricter replication standards?

## Unsupported Claims

- LR-001 does not support any specific CSM-001 parameter choice.
- LR-001 does not prove current alpha.
- LR-001 does not establish production suitability.
- LR-001 does not establish long-only implementation quality.
- LR-001 does not determine whether CSM should be used in any trading system.

## Stage Decision

The literature is sufficiently mature to justify proceeding to CD-001, where one exact construct definition must be frozen before empirical work.
