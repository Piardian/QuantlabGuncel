# Theoretical Foundations

## Strongly Supported

### Regime Switching as Latent State Change
The core theoretical idea is that financial time series sometimes move between persistent states with different means, variances, and covariances. This is the classic Hamilton regime-switching view. [Hamilton 1989](https://www.ssc.wisc.edu/~bhansen/718/Hamilton1989.pdf)

### Time-Varying Risk and Correlation Structure
Many regime papers argue that the market state matters because the risk of assets and their correlations change materially across states. This is especially visible in equity and asset allocation studies. [Regime Changes and Financial Markets](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/timmermann/regime_changes_June_22.pdf), [Asset allocation under multivariate regime switching](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2005/2005-002.pdf)

## Moderately Supported

### Macro and Financial Stress as Regime Drivers
Macroeconomic variables, financial stress, credit conditions, and policy states are often linked to regime transitions, but the exact driver set depends on the study. [How to predict financial stress?](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecb.wp2057.en.pdf), [Predictability of Bull and Bear Markets](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb4/prof/VWL/EWF/Research_Papers/2020-01.pdf)

### Behavioral and Learning Explanations
Some studies interpret regimes as reflecting changing investor beliefs, learning, or behavioral feedback effects. This is plausible and widely discussed, but not uniquely established as the dominant mechanism. [Regime Changes and Financial Markets](https://ideas.repec.org/a/anr/refeco/v4y2012p313-337.html)

## Limited Evidence

### Institutional or Policy Break Mechanisms
Regime transitions are often associated with policy or structural changes, but that association is usually study-specific rather than a universal mechanism.

## Conflicting Evidence

### Single Universal Mechanism
The literature does not support one single mechanism that explains all regime switches across assets, horizons, and decades. The evidence is mixed and context-dependent.
