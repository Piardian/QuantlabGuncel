# Theoretical Foundations

## Portfolio Diversification

Modern portfolio theory treats covariance among assets as central to portfolio risk. Correlation determines whether individual asset risks offset each other or reinforce each other.

Evidence category: Strongly supported by literature.

## Common Factor Dominance

When a market-wide factor dominates security-specific variation, assets move more synchronously. In this setting, average correlations and eigenvalue concentration can rise.

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

## Risk-On / Risk-Off Behavior

Investor behavior can become more synchronized during stress as portfolios are de-risked, leverage is reduced, and exposures are simplified.

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

## Liquidity And Deleveraging

Liquidity shocks and forced selling can increase common movement because market participants sell multiple assets at the same time.

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

## Contagion Versus Interdependence

The literature distinguishes between normal cross-market linkages and crisis-specific transmission. Forbes and Rigobon show that heteroskedasticity can bias correlation-based contagion tests, so observed correlation increases must be interpreted carefully.

Evidence category: Strongly supported as a methodological warning.

## Tail Dependence

Linear correlation may understate dependence during extreme events. Tail-dependence research studies whether assets co-move more strongly in extreme downside states.

Evidence category: Moderately supported, methodology-dependent.

## Systemic Risk

Correlation concentration and principal-component absorption measures are used to describe concentration of common risk. Higher common-factor dominance may indicate reduced diversification and higher systemic vulnerability.

Evidence category: Moderately supported.
