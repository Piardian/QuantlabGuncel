# Empirical Findings

## Scope

This document summarizes reported empirical findings from the literature. It does not independently test predictive validity.

## Finding 1: Correlations Are Time-Varying

Evidence category: Strongly supported.

Dynamic conditional correlation literature and empirical market studies support the view that correlations change over time.

## Finding 2: Correlations Often Rise During Stress

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

Many studies and practitioner reports observe higher co-movement during crisis or stress periods. However, this finding requires caution because volatility changes can bias correlation estimates.

## Finding 3: Bear-Market Dependence Can Be Asymmetric

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

Extreme-correlation research reports that correlations can increase more strongly in downside or bear-market states than in upside states.

## Finding 4: Correlation Spikes Are Not Automatically Contagion

Evidence category: Strongly supported as a methodological warning.

Forbes and Rigobon argue that heteroskedasticity can make standard correlation-based contagion tests misleading.

## Finding 5: Correlation Matrix Estimation Is Noisy

Evidence category: Strongly supported.

High-dimensional covariance and correlation matrices are difficult to estimate from finite samples. Shrinkage methods exist partly because sample covariance matrices can be unstable.

## Finding 6: Common-Factor Concentration Can Describe Systemic Stress

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

PCA/eigenvalue-based approaches interpret higher variance absorption by dominant components as increased common-factor concentration.

## Finding 7: Implied Correlation Represents A Different Dimension

Evidence category: Moderately supported.

Implied correlation indexes attempt to measure market expectations of future index-component co-movement using index and constituent option prices.

## Finding 8: Cross-Asset Correlation Matters For Diversification

Evidence category: Strongly supported.

Changes in equity-bond or cross-asset correlations can materially change portfolio diversification assumptions.
