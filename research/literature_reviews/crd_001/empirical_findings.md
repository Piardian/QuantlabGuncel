# Empirical Findings

## Supported Findings

Credit spreads are widely used as indicators of financial conditions and credit-market stress.

Gilchrist and Zakrajsek show that corporate spreads can be decomposed into expected default-risk and excess bond premium components. Their work reports that the excess bond premium is informative about economic activity.

Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein, and Martin document that changes in credit spreads contain a common systematic component not fully explained by standard credit-risk determinants.

Longstaff, Mithal, and Neis separate default and non-default components in corporate spreads and document that the non-default component varies over time and is related to liquidity and broader bond-market conditions.

Dick-Nielsen, Feldhutter, and Lando document that bond illiquidity became especially important during the subprime crisis period.

Publicly available financial stress measures from Federal Reserve and OFR sources include credit-related information as a meaningful component of broader financial stress.

## Interpretation Boundaries

These findings support Credit Stress as a scientifically meaningful construct. They do not, at the LR stage, establish predictive validity, trading value, or portfolio utility for CRD-001.

## CD-001 Implications

The evidence supports several viable construct-definition paths:

- A simple public spread-level construct.
- A high-yield stress construct.
- A rating-spread construct.
- A model-based excess bond premium construct.
- A composite credit stress construct.

The next stage must select exactly one.
