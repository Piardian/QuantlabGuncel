# Funding Stress Literature Review

## Study Scope

This literature review evaluates Funding Stress as a financial market construct for the Market Signal Discovery Program v3.0.

This stage is descriptive only. It does not define the final construct, select variables for implementation, evaluate predictive validity, or assess economic value.

## Research Questions

1. How is Funding Stress defined in academic and practitioner literature?
2. How is funding liquidity distinguished from market liquidity, credit stress, leverage, and broad financial conditions?
3. Which theoretical mechanisms explain funding stress?
4. Which observable variables are commonly used?
5. Which methodologies are commonly employed?
6. What empirical findings are reported?
7. What limitations are consistently reported?
8. What research gaps remain?

## Executive Finding

Funding Stress is strongly supported by the literature as a distinct and important market construct, but its empirical measurement is materially more difficult than concepts such as price trend or realized volatility.

The literature consistently treats funding liquidity as the ability of institutions or market participants to obtain funding, roll liabilities, finance positions, or meet cash needs without destabilizing cost increases or forced deleveraging. Funding stress occurs when this capacity deteriorates.

The strongest theoretical support comes from funding-market liquidity feedback models, margin constraints, collateral and haircut dynamics, dealer balance-sheet constraints, interbank funding stress, rollover risk, and flight-to-quality behavior.

## Evidence Classification

### Strongly Supported By Literature

- Funding liquidity and market liquidity are linked but conceptually distinct.
- Funding stress can reinforce market stress through margin, collateral, and deleveraging channels.
- Funding conditions are important in crisis dynamics.
- Institutional financial stress indexes commonly include funding-related dimensions.

### Moderately Supported

- Spread-based proxies such as LIBOR-OIS, TED, repo spreads, and related funding spreads are useful indicators of funding stress in specific historical settings.
- Composite indexes can summarize funding stress, but may mix funding, credit, volatility, and liquidity effects.
- Dealer balance-sheet and leverage conditions are relevant to intermediary funding capacity.

### Limited Or Conflicting Evidence

- No single public proxy fully captures funding stress across all periods.
- LIBOR-based proxies became less reliable after changes in unsecured bank funding markets and the LIBOR transition.
- Separating funding stress from credit stress is empirically difficult.

## Key Literature Themes

### Funding Liquidity vs Market Liquidity

Brunnermeier and Pedersen formalize the interaction between traders' funding liquidity and market liquidity. Their central insight is that funding constraints can reduce market-making capacity, which can worsen market liquidity and feed back into funding conditions.

This supports treating funding stress as distinct from, but tightly connected to, market liquidity stress.

### Funding Liquidity Risk

Drehmann and Nikolaou define funding liquidity as the ability to meet demand for money with immediacy, and funding liquidity risk as the possibility this ability deteriorates over a horizon. Their work is especially valuable because it explicitly focuses on definition and measurement.

### Interbank And Wholesale Funding Stress

LIBOR-OIS and TED spreads are widely used in historical literature as proxies for bank funding stress. However, later Federal Reserve research emphasizes that LIBOR became less tightly linked to actual bank funding costs, especially as wholesale unsecured funding markets changed.

### Composite Stress Indexes

The OFR Financial Stress Index includes a funding category alongside credit, equity valuation, safe assets, and volatility. The Chicago Fed NFCI includes many financial variables and decomposes conditions into risk, credit, and leverage components. These institutional tools support funding stress as a recognized dimension, but they also illustrate the risk of mixing related constructs.

### Balance Sheets, Leverage, And Intermediaries

Adrian and Shin emphasize the importance of intermediary balance sheets and leverage. Broker-dealer balance-sheet expansion and contraction can transmit funding conditions into broader financial market behavior.

## Methodological Lessons For CD-001

CD-001 must choose one narrow, reproducible definition. It should not combine every available funding-related proxy into an unfrozen composite unless the theory and measurement rules are specified before implementation.

The key decision for CD-001 is whether FUND-001 should be:

- a spread-based funding stress construct,
- a composite index component construct,
- a dealer/intermediary funding capacity construct,
- a repo/collateral funding construct,
- or a central-bank liquidity operations construct.

Each option is scientifically defensible, but each measures a different part of the funding-stress landscape.

Funding Stress is scientifically mature enough to proceed to CD-001.
