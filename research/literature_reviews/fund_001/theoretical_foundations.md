# FUND-001 / LR-001

# Theoretical Foundations

## Core Theory

Funding Stress is grounded in the idea that financial market participants require financing to hold inventories, provide liquidity, roll liabilities, and maintain leveraged positions. When funding becomes expensive, scarce, or unreliable, market behavior can change even without an immediate change in fundamental asset value.

## Funding-Market Liquidity Feedback

The most important theoretical foundation is the feedback between funding liquidity and market liquidity.

When funding conditions worsen:

- traders and dealers reduce positions,
- market-making capacity falls,
- bid-ask spreads can widen,
- price impact can increase,
- asset prices may decline,
- collateral values fall,
- margins and haircuts may rise,
- funding conditions worsen further.

This loop supports the interpretation of funding stress as a systemic amplification mechanism.

Evidence status: Strongly supported by theoretical literature.

## Margin And Haircut Channel

Funding stress can appear when margin requirements or collateral haircuts increase. This reduces effective leverage and can force deleveraging.

This channel is especially relevant in leveraged trading, repo markets, derivatives markets, and dealer balance-sheet activity.

Evidence status: Strongly supported theoretically; empirical measurement is more difficult.

## Dealer Balance-Sheet Channel

Dealers and financial intermediaries provide risk-bearing and liquidity services. When balance-sheet capacity tightens, intermediaries may reduce liquidity provision and risk transfer.

This theory connects funding stress to:

- broker-dealer leverage,
- repo financing,
- inventory capacity,
- market-making behavior,
- systemic liquidity.

Evidence status: Moderately to strongly supported.

## Rollover Risk Channel

Institutions dependent on short-term borrowing face rollover risk. If lenders refuse to renew funding or demand higher compensation, liquidity stress can arise quickly.

This is central to interbank stress, wholesale funding stress, and crisis-period funding pressure.

Evidence status: Strongly supported conceptually.

## Counterparty Risk Channel

Funding spreads may widen because lenders demand compensation for counterparty default risk. This is important because some funding proxies, such as LIBOR-OIS, reflect both liquidity risk and credit risk.

Evidence status: Strongly supported as a measurement complication.

## Flight-To-Quality And Flight-To-Liquidity

During stress, investors often move toward safer and more liquid assets. This can raise demand for safe collateral and worsen funding pressure for riskier borrowers.

Evidence status: Moderately supported.

## Central-Bank Backstop Channel

Funding stress can trigger demand for central-bank liquidity facilities. Some literature measures funding stress using open-market operation behavior or facility usage.

Evidence status: Supported, but implementation is jurisdiction-specific.

## Implication For FUND-001

Funding Stress is not merely high volatility, low liquidity, or credit stress. It is a financing-capacity construct. However, observed proxies frequently contain overlapping information from volatility, liquidity, and credit.

This makes CD-001 especially important.
